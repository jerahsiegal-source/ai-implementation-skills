Who are you, as far as the U.S. government is concerned?

You have a legal name, a tax ID, and a Social Security or immigration number. You may also have a voter registration, a driver's license, a passport, or a government employment card.

None of these connect to each other. Your tax ID has nothing to do with your driver's license, and your Social Security number won't let you drive a car. Most of us juggle all these separate pieces without thinking about it much. Could it be simpler?

Estonia says yes.

Since it regained independence in 1991, Estonia has become a leader in digital services. [The Atlantic](http://www.theatlantic.com/international/archive/2014/01/lessons-from-the-worlds-most-tech-savvy-government/283341/) called it "the world's most tech-savvy government." Last week, a group of Estonia's best-known technologists spent a week in D.C., meeting with the State Department, the World Bank, and U.S. digital services teams.

*U.S. Chief Technology Officer Megan Smith, members of U.S. digital services teams, and the representatives from Estonia.*

At 18F, we were glad to sit down with them to talk about digital services, cyber security, and IT funding. The timing was a coincidence: the meeting came just after a massive [U.S. security breach](http://www.google.com/url?q=http%3A%2F%2Fwww.opm.gov%2Fnews%2Freleases%2F2015%2F06%2Fopm-to-notify-employees-of-cybersecurity-incident%2F&sa=D&sntz=1&usg=AFQjCNHJUgo7egn0XNVaECpcNNSdUYnVZQ) exposed the information of about 4 million federal employees.

Estonia's Information System Authority does three main jobs. It runs the digital government ([e-Estonia](https://e-estonia.com/)), handles cyber security, and manages government and public IT funds.

"It's quite a unique system we have," said Liina Areng, the agency's head of international relations. She pointed out how much it matters that the people who build the systems and the people who fund them work in the same agency.

The system has won praise around the world. It protects the identities of about 1.3 million citizens and offers online services few countries can match. Estonians can change their legal names, register property purchases, and file taxes online. Only about 120 legal interactions with the government still have to be done on paper.

"We have lost the ability to go back to paper," said Andres Kütt, chief architect of Estonia's Information System Authority. "We don't have physical rooms to store the paper."
